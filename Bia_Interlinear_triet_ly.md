# Interlinear · 夾注

**A visual philosophy of the second voice — the dense commentary that lives between the lines.**

Interlinear grows from an old scholarly habit: a sparse primary text set in generous cells, and between its columns a second, smaller, tireless voice — the gloss, the annotation, the reader's patient argument with the page. Form is built from this doubling. Large calm units stand like pillars; in the narrow channels beside them, marks gather in paired threads, twice as many, half as large, impossibly regular. The work must look as if a scholar with a fine brush spent a season filling those channels by hand — meticulously crafted, every mark weighed, nothing hurried.

Direction matters. The composition reads in vertical columns, from right to left, so the eye travels the way a classical page asks it to and arrives last at the quietest margin. Space is not evenly shared: primary cells breathe, interlinear channels are compressed and saturated, and the outer margins remain almost empty, giving the whole block the presence of an object on a table. The tension between openness and density is the composition, and it must be balanced with master-level precision rather than decoration.

Colour is the colour of a reading desk after long use: warm aged paper, carbon ink diluted into a disciplined ladder of greys, and vermilion reserved exclusively for the reader's hand — small circles and dots of punctuation placed where meaning turns. Vermilion never fills a shape; it only marks. A single muted ochre may appear once, as a seal of completion. The restraint should feel earned, as though every value had been tested against the paper under different light, the product of deep expertise and painstaking attention.

Typography behaves like a cataloguer's notation: hairline numerals, a thin monospaced index, one pale specimen glyph allowed to stand at architectural scale but never louder than the ink. Words do not explain; they locate. Labels sit exactly on their rules, aligned to the same invisible grid as the marks, and never touch or crowd another element.

Hierarchy comes from accumulation and annotation, not size. The densest channel pulls the eye first, the vermilion reading marks second, the threshold rules third, the marginal index last. The finished plate should feel like a page from an imaginary treatise on how understanding deepens — proof that the invisible work of reading closely can be drawn, counted and preserved, through an execution so careful it seems to have taken countless hours.

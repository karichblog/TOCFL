# Grid Sediment · 稿紙沉積

**A visual philosophy of slow accumulation on a ruled ground.**

Grid Sediment begins from the humblest object in a literate culture: the squared manuscript sheet, where every character is given one cell and nothing more. The ground is never blank paper; it is paper already ruled, already expecting. Form arises only by filling cells — one mark per square, never spilling, never borrowing space from a neighbour. Composition is therefore not drawn but *deposited*: columns thicken from the bottom up like strata in a cliff face, and the eye reads time as height. Every placement must feel meticulously considered, as though a master calligrapher had spent weeks deciding which single square should receive the next drop of ink.

Space is disciplined and generous at once. The grid occupies a measured field with wide, quiet margins, so the accumulation has air around it and reads as an object, not a pattern. Empty cells are not absence — they are the unfilled future, given the same hairline care as the filled past. Rhythm comes from repetition with minute variance: identical squares whose ink density shifts by small, painstaking increments, so that from afar a column is a solid tone and up close it is hundreds of individual decisions. This duality — monumental at arm's length, intimate at reading distance — is the core of the work and must be executed with master-level precision.

Colour is restricted to what a scholar's desk would hold: warm unbleached paper, carbon-black ink thinned to many greys, one deep lacquer red reserved for moments of structure, and a single muted ochre used sparingly as a seal. Red never decorates; it marks rules, thresholds, the places where the system declares something. The palette should feel calibrated rather than chosen, as if every value had been mixed, tested and re-mixed across countless sittings.

Typography is a whisper in the margins, behaving like the notations of a surveyor: thin monospaced numerals, hairline rules, tiny labels anchored precisely to the lines they describe. Text never explains; it indexes. A single glyph may appear larger, but only as a specimen — pale, airy, part of the architecture rather than a headline. Nothing overlaps, nothing crowds the edge; every label sits on its baseline with the exactness of an engraved scale.

Hierarchy emerges from mass, not size. The heaviest accumulation draws the eye first, the threshold lines second, the marginal notation last. The finished piece should read as a scientific plate from an imaginary discipline — the cartography of patience — and should look like the product of deep expertise and painstaking attention: an artifact that proves something as invisible as gradual understanding can be measured, cell by cell, and laid down in ink.
